# Product

## Register

product

## Users

Glyph Studio is for visual creators, designers, social editors, and technically curious artists who want to turn images or short videos into export-ready ASCII and glyph-based visuals. They arrive with a source asset, experiment with render style, tune motion or finish passes, then export a polished still or animation.

## Product Purpose

The product turns media into controllable ASCII artwork without making users fight a technical pipeline. Success means the creator can understand the current source, see the render clearly, adjust the most important controls in a natural sequence, and export with confidence.

## Brand Personality

Precise, orbital, disciplined. The interface should feel like mission hardware for image conversion: calm under pressure, technically sharp, and quietly cinematic.

## Anti-references

Avoid generic neon cyberpunk dashboards, glassy SaaS panels, cluttered desktop photo editors, toy-like ASCII demos, and landing-page theatrics. Do not bury the live preview behind decorative UI. Do not make every control look equally important.

## Design Principles

Preview is mission control. The live canvas is the center of the experience; controls orbit it and never compete with it.

Workflow beats feature inventory. Upload, calibrate, shape motion, and export should read as a clear sequence even when many controls exist.

Precision without friction. Dense controls are allowed, but labels, groupings, and states must make adjustment feel deliberate.

Instrumental beauty. The Starlink influence should come through in dark orbital surfaces, crisp telemetry, restrained light, and exact alignment, not in decorative space art.

Export confidence. The final step should make format, resolution, timing, and active effects legible before the user commits.

## Accessibility & Inclusion

Target WCAG AA contrast for text and controls. Preserve keyboard focus rings, visible labels, semantic buttons, and screen-reader names for icon buttons. Provide reduced-motion-safe UI transitions and avoid relying on hover-only affordances.
